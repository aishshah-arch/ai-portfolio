How a Neural Network Learns to Spot a Cat

A hands-on, no-math explanation of neural networks for anyone new to AI. Click, slide, and train a tiny network yourself.

Created by Aishwarya Shah · Indiana Wesleyan University · November 2026

A neural network is a computer system that learns to recognize patterns by practicing on lots of examples, the same way you learned to recognize a cat as a child: not from a rulebook, but from seeing many cats.
1. The three rooms2. Volume knobs3. Learning from mistakes4. Why it matters
Step 1

A team of tiny decision-makers

Picture a factory with three rooms. A photo comes in on one side, gets examined by teams of small workers called neurons, and a decision comes out the other side. Press the button to send a photo through.

📥 Input roomReceives the raw information, like the pixels of a photo.
🧠 Hidden roomsEach worker looks for one simple clue. Early workers spot edges and colors; later ones combine them into "pointy ears" or "whiskers."
📤 Output roomMakes the final call, such as "90% sure this is a cat."
Step 2

Not every clue matters equally

Each clue has a weight, like a volume knob: turned up, the clue counts a lot; turned down, it barely counts; turned negative, it argues against "cat." Then an activation function acts like a doorbell that only rings if it is pressed hard enough. Pick an animal and adjust the knobs.

The dashed line is the doorbell's threshold. Try it: can you set the knobs so the cat rings the bell but the fox doesn't?
Step 3

Learning from mistakes

Nobody sets the knobs by hand in a real network. They start random, so the network guesses badly. After each guess, it checks the right answer and measures how wrong it was. Two things happen next. First, backpropagation works backward through the network to figure out how much each knob contributed to the mistake. Then an optimizer (a common one is called gradient descent) nudges each knob a little in the direction that would have helped. Press "Practice one round" and watch the accuracy climb.

      Step 4

      Why it matters

      Real networks have millions or billions of knobs and many hidden rooms. When there are many layers, we call it deep learning. Different designs suit different jobs:

      📱 Face unlockImage-focused networks (CNNs) find patterns in pixels, much like our cat detector.
      🩻 Medical imagingNetworks help flag possible disease in X-rays for a doctor to review.
      🗣️ Voice assistantsNetworks with memory (RNNs) handle sequences like speech, word by word.
      🎨 Generated imagesTwo networks compete (GANs): one creates, one critiques, and both improve.
      One honest limitation: a network can be very accurate without being able to explain why it decided something. This is called the "black box" problem (Jones, 2021), and it is why people still review AI decisions in high-stakes areas like healthcare.
      Quick check: if a network keeps mistaking foxes for cats, what does it need more of?

      More varied, correctly labeled examples of both foxes and cats, and then a test on photos it has never seen. Variety matters: foxes of different sizes and colors, cats of different breeds, in different poses and lighting. Simply adding more pictures that look like the ones it already has can reinforce the same weakness. You can see this in Step 3: after training, the network gets every practice animal right, but the "Test on animals it has never seen" button shows it still mistakes a fennec fox for a cat, because nothing it practiced on looked like a small fox. Real networks learn many more clues from the pixels themselves, and varied examples are what push them to find the clues that actually tell a fox from a cat.
